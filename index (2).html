1. Qubits, superposition and measurement

A classical bit is 0 or 1, like a light switch. A qubit can be a blend of both, written |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩. This blend is superposition. α and β are amplitudes. When we measure, we get only 0 (probability |α|²) or 1 (probability |β|²), and the qubit collapses to that result.

|α|² + |β|² = 1   (normalization)
Real life: A spinning coin is neither heads nor tails. When it lands (measurement) you see just one. A qubit is a spinning coin with exact numbers attached.
Example: |ψ⟩ = 0.6|0⟩ + 0.8|1⟩P(0) = 0.6² = 0.36 = 36%. P(1) = 0.8² = 0.64 = 64%. Sum = 1 ✓. After measuring 1, the next measurement gives 1 with 100%.
Example: find β if α = 1/2(1/2)² + β² = 1, so β² = 3/4 and β = √3/2 ≈ 0.866.

3. Interference

Amplitudes can be negative. When two paths lead to the same result, their amplitudes add (constructive) or cancel (destructive). Quantum algorithms arrange this so wrong answers cancel and right ones grow. Circuit: H, then a phase φ on |1⟩, then H.

P(0) = cos²(φ/2)
Real life: Noise-cancelling headphones add an opposite sound wave to cancel noise. Colours in soap bubbles are also interference.
Example: φ = 90°P(0) = cos²(45°) = 0.5 → 50%. At φ = 0° P(0)=100%, at φ = 180° P(0)=0%.

4. Superposition and entanglement

Two qubits can be entangled: they share one state that cannot be split into separate states. The Bell state is (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2. Each qubit alone is random, but the two results always match, even far apart.

Real life: A pair of magic gloves. Each glove goes in a sealed box. Opening one shows "left", so you instantly know the other is "right". In quantum physics the outcome is not fixed in advance, yet the match is still perfect. No message is sent, so no faster-than-light communication.
Example: Bell state probabilitiesP(00) = (1/√2)² = 1/2, P(11) = 1/2, P(01) = P(10) = 0. If Alice gets 1, Bob gets 1 with 100% certainty. Independent |+⟩|+⟩ gives each of 00, 01, 10, 11 a probability of 1/4.

5. No-cloning theorem

It is impossible to build a machine that copies an unknown quantum state. Proof idea: copying would need U|ψ⟩|0⟩ = |ψ⟩|ψ⟩, but quantum operations are linear.

Example: why copying |+⟩ failsA true copy gives |+⟩|+⟩ = (|00⟩+|01⟩+|10⟩+|11⟩)/2. Linearity (copy |0⟩ and |1⟩ separately, then add) gives (|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2. These are different, so no copier exists. (Known states can be re-prepared; only unknown ones cannot be copied.)
Real life: You can photocopy a document, but you can never photocopy a live spinning coin without stopping it. This also makes quantum money and quantum cryptography secure, since an eavesdropper cannot copy the qubits.

6. Quantum teleportation

Teleportation moves the state of a qubit (not matter) using one shared entangled pair plus 2 classical bits. Steps: (1) Alice and Bob share a Bell pair. (2) Alice does CNOT and H on her unknown qubit and her half. (3) She measures both and gets two bits. (4) She sends the bits normally. (5) Bob applies X if the second bit is 1, then Z if the first is 1. Alice's original is destroyed, so no cloning happens.

Real life: Like faxing a design: the original is erased, and the copy appears after the paper (the 2 bits) arrives. Without the bits, Bob's qubit looks random.
Example: the 4 outcomesEach of the bits 00, 01, 10, 11 occurs with probability 1/4. Correction: 00 → nothing, 01 → X, 10 → Z, 11 → X then Z.

7. Quantum cryptography (BB84)

Alice sends random bits, each in a random basis (+ or ×). Bob measures in random bases. They publicly compare bases and keep bits where bases matched (about 50%). If Eve intercepts, she must guess bases, which disturbs the qubits and creates about 25% errors that Alice and Bob can detect.

Real life: A letter in a special envelope that changes colour if someone peeks. Banks use quantum key distribution over fibre this way.
Example: why 25%?Eve picks the wrong basis with probability 1/2. Then Bob (when bases match) errs with probability 1/2. Error = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 = 25%. Without Eve the error is 0%.

8. Grover's algorithm

Grover searches an unsorted list of N items in about (π/4)√N steps, while a classical search needs about N/2 on average. Each step: (1) oracle flips the sign of the target's amplitude, (2) diffusion reflects all amplitudes about their average. The target amplitude grows slowly.

Real life: Finding one name in an unsorted phone book of 1,000,000 entries: about 500,000 checks classically, but only about 785 Grover steps.
Example: N = 8Optimal steps = (π/4)√8 ≈ 2.22 → 2. With sinθ = 1/√8, θ ≈ 0.361 rad, success = sin²(5θ) = sin²(1.807) ≈ 94.5%. Doing a 3rd step overshoots and success drops.

9. Shor's algorithm

Shor factors large numbers fast, which would break RSA encryption. The quantum part finds the period r of f(x) = aˣ mod N. Then, if r is even, the factors are gcd(a^(r/2) − 1, N) and gcd(a^(r/2) + 1, N). The rest is classical.

Real life: Multiplying two big primes is easy, un-multiplying is hard, like mixing paint. Shor "un-mixes" it using period finding.
Example: factor 15 with a = 77¹=7, 7²=49 mod 15=4, 7³=28 mod 15=13, 7⁴=91 mod 15=1 → r = 4. 7² = 49 → gcd(48,15)=3 and gcd(50,15)=5. So 15 = 3 × 5.

10. Quantum supremacy

Quantum supremacy means a quantum computer does a task that no classical computer can do in a reasonable time (the task need not be useful). n qubits need 2ⁿ amplitudes to describe, so classical simulation explodes. In 2019 Google's 53-qubit Sycamore ran a random-circuit sampling task, claiming about 200 seconds against thousands of years classically. Classical teams later challenged the estimate, so the debate continues.

Real life: Each extra qubit doubles the work, like folding paper: 42 folds reach the Moon.
Example: 53 qubits2⁵³ ≈ 9.0 × 10¹⁵ amplitudes × 16 bytes ≈ 144 petabytes of memory, far beyond any laptop.

11. Quantum tunneling

A particle can pass through an energy barrier it classically could not climb, because its wavefunction leaks into the barrier. The chance falls exponentially with width: T ≈ e−2κL. For an electron facing a 1 eV barrier, κ ≈ 5.12 nm⁻¹. Tunneling powers the Sun's fusion, flash memory and the scanning tunneling microscope, and superconducting qubits use it too.

Real life: A ball rolling at a hill that is too tall sometimes appears on the other side. For tiny particles and thin barriers, this actually happens.
Example: L = 0.5 nmT = e^(−2 × 5.12 × 0.5) = e^(−5.12) ≈ 0.006 = 0.6%. At L = 0.3 nm T ≈ 4.6%. Halving the width helps enormously.
